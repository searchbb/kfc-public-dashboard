# 软件工程生产力：你衡量的对象错了

[p002]
*Laks Alagappan**, VP & Client Engagement Manager at Genpact, leads enterprise technology delivery for Tier 1 banks across the UK and Europe.*

[p003]
For the past two years, software engineering has been awash with AI productivity claims. One enterprise might say AI cuts development time by a third, another might claim it doubles output and all the while, vendors promise even larger gains. Every organization cites a different number, each sounding equally definitive.

[p004]
Every CIO wants faster delivery, and every CFO wants lower costs. However, the variation in these claims should be the first clue that we're measuring productivity incorrectly.

[p005]
One of the most rigorous studies to date told a different story. In 2025, the AI safety research nonprofit METR conducted a randomized controlled trial with 16 experienced open-source developers working on mature codebases they knew well. Participants expected AI to make them about 25% faster and still believed it had afterwards. Objective measurement showed they were [about 19% slower](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/).

[p006]
A subsequent 2026 METR study [suggested](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/#wider-adoption-of-ai-has-made-it-more-difficult-to-measure-task-level-productivity) that newer AI tools were likely delivering greater productivity gains, although selection effects made the size of that improvement difficult to measure reliably.

[p007]
That gap between perceived and measured productivity is the real insight. The question isn't which productivity figure is correct but, rather, what it's productive at, how it's measured and against what baseline it's measured. Two organizations can both be right because they're measuring entirely different things.

[p008]
## Software engineering is a system, not a coding activity.

[p009]
The industry has unintentionally reduced software engineering to coding.

[p010]
In reality, software engineering is the discipline of transforming business intent into secure, reliable and compliant software running in production. Coding is only one activity within a broader life cycle that includes requirements engineering, architecture, testing, security, governance, deployment, operations and continuous improvement.

[p011]
AI has made remarkable progress in accelerating code generation, but improving one activity doesn't necessarily improve the performance of the entire engineering system. The constraint simply moves elsewhere.

[p012]
Google's "2025 DORA State of AI-Assisted Software Development" report [discovered](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/announcing-the-2025-dora-report) that AI adoption was associated with higher software delivery throughput but lower delivery stability. Without equally mature testing, code review, release engineering and operational discipline, organizations risk accelerating defects rather than business outcomes.

[p013]
## Optimizing one stage doesn't optimize the system.

[p014]
Many organizations still evaluate AI using local metrics such as coding speed, lines of code generated, AI suggestion acceptance rates and individual developer output. These metrics, while useful, aren't measures of software engineering productivity.

[p015]
Enterprise software delivery is a complex system. Improving one stage rarely improves the performance of the whole. Faster implementation alone will have limited impact if requirements take weeks to clarify, testing remains manual, environments are difficult to provision or governance delays production releases.

[p016]
This is a pattern many banking technology leaders report. AI improves individual developer productivity, and dashboards look encouraging, yet release cadence changes little because the real constraints remain change approvals, regression testing, compliance reviews, environment provisioning and operational readiness.

[p017]
Organizations should measure software engineering by the speed, quality and reliability with which it delivers business outcomes—not by how quickly code is produced.

[p018]
## AI's biggest opportunities exist beyond coding.

[p019]
Some of AI's greatest opportunities exist before a single line of code is written and in the less visible work surrounding it: analyzing and de-conflicting business requirements, generating architectural options, assessing regulatory impacts, estimating delivery effort, mapping application dependencies, producing documentation, automating test creation and investigating production incidents.

[p020]
These activities often benefit more from AI than code generation itself, improving speed, consistency and quality while preventing defects earlier and at far lower cost.

[p021]
A DORA report [noted](https://dora.dev/ai/gen-ai-report/dora-impact-of-generative-ai-in-software-development.pdf) that AI's benefits scale with the maturity of the surrounding engineering system. Teams with strong automated testing, disciplined engineering practices and rapid feedback loops translate AI-assisted throughput into better business outcomes. Teams without those foundations simply generate more code—and often more instability.

[p022]
AI amplifies engineering discipline; it doesn't replace it.

[p023]
## Productivity doesn't automatically mean fewer developers.

[p024]
One of the boldest predictions surrounding AI is that dramatically fewer software engineers will be needed. While some organizations may have cited AI-assisted development as one factor in workforce reductions, the evidence that coding assistants alone will enable large-scale headcount reductions remains limited.

[p025]
Coding is only one component of software engineering. Unless AI also removes constraints across requirements, architecture, testing, security, governance and release management, expecting coding assistants alone to eliminate large numbers of engineering roles remains more aspiration than reality.

[p026]
[Jevons Paradox](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0301421508007428) tells us that making something cheaper often increases demand rather than reducing it. In software engineering, productivity gains have historically enabled organizations to build more products, modernize technical debt, improve security and pursue previously uneconomic initiatives. Rather than shrinking engineering teams, they've often expanded what organizations choose to build.

[p027]
Whether AI becomes primarily a cost-reduction or capacity-expansion tool will mainly depend on leadership strategy. The technology enables both outcomes.

[p028]
## The software engineer's role is changing.

[p029]
As AI becomes embedded across the software engineering life cycle, developers will spend less time writing routine code and more time governing how software is engineered. Their focus will increasingly shift toward system design, architecture, AI orchestration, security, quality engineering and establishing guardrails that ensure AI-generated software is secure, compliant, reliable and aligned with business objectives.

[p030]
Software engineering is becoming less about producing code and more about governing intelligent systems that produce software responsibly.

[p031]
## Measure what actually matters.

[p032]
Which productivity number is right? Probably all of them—and none of them.

[p033]
Each reflects a local measurement within a different engineering system. If organizations expect coding assistants alone to transform software delivery, many will be disappointed. When AI is combined with modern engineering practices, platform automation, DevSecOps, governance and organizational change, significant gains become plausible.

[p034]
The real transformation is software engineering evolving into a discipline where humans design systems, govern AI, assure quality and solve business problems.

[p035]
The question is no longer "How much faster can developers write code?" It's "How much faster—and more reliably—can our engineering system deliver meaningful business outcomes?" Until organizations answer that question with system-level metrics rather than coding benchmarks, every headline productivity figure—however impressive—will remain true for someone and incomplete for everyone else.
